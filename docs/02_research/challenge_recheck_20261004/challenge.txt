01 Background As of 2024, 2.6 billion people—about one-third of the world’s population—remain offline, and internet use ranges from over 90% in high-income countries to just 27% in low-income countries. Meanwhile, 800 million people still lack official ID, limiting access to digital public services, finance, and healthcare. Closing that divide is the mandate of the World Bank Group's Digital & AI Vice Presidency, which supports countries to expand affordable, inclusive broadband connectivity, bring people onto digitally enabled services, and bring more women online, commitments tracked on the World Bank Group Scorecard. Employment is the second challenge. Over the next decade, an estimated 1.2 billion young people in the Global South will reach working age, while current trends are projected to generate only 420 million jobs. Artificial intelligence and the broader data revolution are expanding what institutions can do, improving service delivery, and opening new economic pathways. Yet not everyone is benefiting equally. This challenge sits inside that agenda. Most AI applications have been deployed and designed with the assumption of baseline access to reliable connectivity, use of a smart-model device, and a user already familiar with how to prompt when interacting with an AI interface. This challenge is designed for the many settings where these conditions are either strained, limited, or intermittent. 
02  What is Small AI Small AI is a design approach for AI development that promotes targeted, use-case-driven AI: eg. while general-purpose large language models continue to improve their viability in constrained environments, a Small AI approach focuses on leveraging AI capabilities — pattern recognition, text generation, or the ability to understand context1 — to work on a single, well-defined problem, on a device easily accessible to users. That device may be a basic phone, or a small piece of on-device hardware such as a sensor or a microcontroller that runs the model locally. The AI use case may be predictive, generative, or agentic; what matters is that its targeted use, less reliance on the existence of an extensive data-center infrastructure, functions where connectivity is weak or absent, can be trained or tuned on local data, and centers the user through common interfaces such as SMS and voice. The design is governed by the operating constraint and the problem to be solved, not by the scale or capability of the model. This comes with an important caveat. A good AI solution is not sufficient on its own. Small AI depends on a set of foundational enablers — adequate connectivity, digital literacy, and trusted local institutions — and without them even the best tool remains a pilot. Each sector brief below therefore sets out these preconditions alongside the challenges specific to the sector, so that a proposed AI solution can demonstrate a clear and distinct value add. Applicants should address one sectoral challenge to build their Small AI solution.

03  What is the Small AI for Development Hackathon? In this regard, the World Bank Group Digital & AI Vice Presidency, in collaboration with the Youth Summit, Development Economics Vice Presidency (DEC), Information and Technology Solutions (ITS) and Hack-nation, is organizing a hackathon to bring the youth perspective into how AI is developed. Building on the World Bank Group's work to help countries adopt and scale AI, including the AI Repository, a catalogue of AI use cases and the lessons from deploying them. Over one weekend, applicants will build a working Small AI solution to one of the sector challenges below. Through their Small AI solution, applicants should seek to answer: what does localizing AI development mean for you? Young people are often closest to the challenges this competition addresses, and their perspective on how technology should respond is essential. This challenge invites young people to consider what it means to develop AI for their own context, language, and local needs, and to reflect candidly on both the opportunities and the risks. Entries that acknowledge these trade-offs are encouraged. 
04  Eligibility, Timeline and Logistics The competition is open to entrants aged 18 to 35, in line with the United Nations’ definition of youth, open to participants from over 150 countries. Applicants from emerging economies are encouraged to apply. Key dates • Application Opens: September 2026 • Competition weekend: 3–4 October 2026 • Shortlist selection by expert evaluators: 5–6 October • Ignite Talk by the winners in Seoul: 21 October 2026  Entries submitted during the competition weekend will be reviewed by expert evaluators drawn from health, agriculture, and tourism, who will select the shortlist for final judging. Each sector panel selects a shortlist of finalists in its own sector, and one winning team is chosen per sector, three winners in total. One representative from each of the three winning teams, one from each sector, will be invited to Seoul to present at the Global AI and Digital Summit 2026, co-hosted by the World Bank Group and the Government of Korea from 19 to 22 October. The Summit convenes governments, the private sector, investors, and international organizations to examine how practical, fit-for-purpose AI can create jobs and expand opportunities in developing economies, with dedicated attention to health, agriculture, tourism, education, and government services. The Ignite Talks will take place in a breakout session. Organizers will facilitate networking opportunities and, where feasible, support introductions to investors, private-sector representatives, MSMEs, and government officials attending the event. Travel expenses will be covered. Visa arrangements will be communicated to each winning team directly (one representative per team will be traveling).

06  The Rules Be creative, think out of the box, whatever form your solution takes: • it runs on a device the user already has; • its core feature works offline; • its model files are small enough to side-load or send over a weak connection; • at least one interaction is in a local language, by voice or text — name the language, and expect to be asked how the tool would fare in a less-supported one.  AI Guardrails —  Human-in-the-loop principles: A person makes the final call. The tool informs a decision and flags what it is unsure of; it does not act on the user's behalf. If an entry proposes an agentic workflow, ensure the AI checks in with user appropriately. —  Avoid hallucinations.

10  Glossary TERM  ·  WHAT IT MEANS Small AI Small AI is a design approach for AI development that promotes targeted, use-case-driven AI: e.g. while general-purpose large language models continue to improve their viability in constrained environments, a Small AI approach focuses on leveraging AI capabilities — pattern recognition, text generation, or the ability to understand context — to work on a single, well-defined problem, on a device easily accessible to users. Offline / on-device (edge) AI Running the model on the user's own phone or hardware instead of a server, so it works with no connection Fine-tuning Adapting an existing, pre-trained model to your case — your language, your crop — by training it further on a smaller, focused dataset. Faster and cheaper than building one from scratch. Quantization Shrinking a model so it fits on a basic device or downloads over a weak link. Store-and-forward Save it now, send it later. The record waits on the phone until a signal appears. Dataset A collection of examples — recordings, photographs, messages, survey answers — that a model learns from or is tested against.

Annex A. Health 
A.1 The scenario Noor has a clinic near where she lives, but it is overcrowded and the quality of care is not always of a good standard. Doctors are well-meaning but are not always up to date with the latest medical guidance. The significant patient load, coupled with burdensome record-keeping requirements, means that they are not able to give each patient the attention that is needed. • The problem. Access to primary care in remote catchments is limited by clinician scarcity and the absence of on-site diagnostics. Patients travel long distances to be seen, health workers have limited time per case, and low patient digital literacy compounds both. • What other digital tools have been in use. Basic mobile technology has delivered measurable health gains so far. The Mwana project in Zambia and Malawi ran entirely on SMS: built on the RapidSMS platform, it transmitted infant HIV test results from central laboratories back to rural clinics and sent automated appointment reminders to mothers, cutting result turnaround times sharply. Uganda's mTrac used the same SMS foundation to collect weekly data from health facilities into the national health information system and to push medicine stock-out alerts, reaching over 10 million subscribers. Both worked through message routing and data aggregation. The Broadband Commission estimated that wider mHealth adoption in Sub-Saharan Africa could save approximately one million lives over five years against malaria, HIV/AIDS, and perinatal conditions. • Where AI can add value. The clinical use of AI in low-resource health with the strongest evidence base is image-based screening. In 2021, the World Health Organization endorsed computer-aided detection (CAD) software for chest X-ray interpretation in tuberculosis screening in individuals aged 15 and older, based on the evaluation of three products — CAD4TB, Lunit INSIGHT CXR, and qXR — that performed comparably to, and in later versions better than, human readers. These tools are now deployed in active case-finding programmes across India, the Philippines, and some countries in Africa, often paired with ultra-portable X-ray hardware. AI-enabled retinal screening for diabetic retinopathy and visual inspection tools for cervical pre-cancer follow the same pattern: the model reads an image and flags the cases that warrant a clinician's review. • The risks. The World Health Organization (WHO)'s Ethics and governance of artificial intelligence for health is explicit that opportunities and risks are linked, and cautions against unethical collection of health data, biases encoded in algorithms, and risks to patient safety and cybersecurity. Models trained largely on data from high-income countries can underperform on the populations they are then deployed to. Bias can be encoded silently and reproduced across many patients before it is detected. Patient data moving through a mobile network raises safety exposures that a paper record does not. A confident wrong output where a life is at stake is worse than no output. Applicants should design for these risks from the start. • Preconditions to keep in mind. International Finance Corporation (IFC)'s TechEmerge Health program covered Kenya, Uganda, and Ethiopia. Even its strongest AI-assisted ECG and diagnostic tools needed 2G/3G to work. Clinician trust and regulatory acceptance took years to build before anything scaled. Health

Annex B. Agriculture B1. Scenario Noor’s coffee yields have dropped this season, and she cannot say why. The nearest extension officer visits the sub-county twice a year at best, and at harvest she sells her parchment to whichever middleman drives up the valley, at whatever price he names. • The problem. Smallholder farmers lack timely, localized advice on what is affecting their crop, and they lack independent information on what that crop is worth when it is time to sell. Public extension services are constrained by staff shortages, manual data collection, and delayed alerts. In many settings, the binding constraint is the absence of a working farmer registry rather than the absence of an algorithm; without a registry, farmers cannot be reached at scale by any tool, digital or otherwise. • What other digital tools have been in use. In Côte d'Ivoire, the World Bank has supported digital platforms that connect farmers to advisory content and to markets, registering producers and delivering price and input information through the web and mobile channels. The World Bank Group's AgriConnect initiative aims to reach 300 million farmers and double annual agribusiness investment to $9 billion by 2030. • Where AI can add value. India's Wadhwani AI cotton pest tool uses computer vision: a farmer photographs a pheromone trap, and the model counts and identifies the pests on the image, then advises whether and when to spray. That is pattern recognition across thousands of visual examples, compressed to run on a basic smartphone offline. Voice-based advisory in local languages is a second line of promising work, particularly where literacy or screen literacy is a constraint. • Preconditions to keep in mind. In Ukraine, a working farmer registry unlocked advisory services, insurance, and grants for 150,000 farmers. AI stacked on absent registries, absent phones, or absent trust in the advisory system will be difficult to implement in practice. Annex C. Tourism C.1 The scenario  Noor's farm is not marketed on a digital platform. The visitors who find it arrive and have help translating through a local guide, and she has no way of knowing what value she created, what worked during the tourists’ visits and what did not once they leave. • The problem. Tourism is a major job creator in the economies where Noor lives, and small operators are where most of that employment sits. Their ability to hire and grow depends on customers they can reach and on understanding the value those customers actually take home. Most small operators run on instinct: they know visitors leave happy, but not why, not which parts of the experience are worth building on, and not how to turn a good review into a returning customer, a referral, or a new product. Weak digital presence and marketing that rarely extends past word of mouth compound the gap. • What other digital tools have been in use. In Jordan, the World Bank has supported work to bring youth and small operators onto digital platforms and into online services, including training operators to list and manage their business on booking and marketing platforms. Getting an operator visible online with a listing, a booking page, digital payments, already unlocks customers without any AI. • Where AI can add value. Small language models can read across a scattered set of reviews and messages and tell the operator what visitors keep returning to, what they wish had been different, and which parts of the experience are worth building the next product around; the analytical work most small operators cannot do for themselves. • Pre-conditions to keep in mind. In Jordan, the binding constraint for informal operators was the absence of a digital listing and the skills to manage one.  Hackathon challenge Design and demonstrate a Small AI solution that helps Noor, as a small tourism operator, complete one meaningful business workflow; for example, becoming discoverable, communicating across languages, responding to enquiries, shaping an experience, managing a booking, learning from vistor feedback or following up with a guest.
